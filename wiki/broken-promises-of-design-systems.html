The Broken Promises of Design Systems

Design systems cannot guarantee great products, effortless adoption, or universal scale; their defensible promise is narrower—to encode stable decisions, reduce repeated work, and preserve room for contextual judgment.

The maximalist promise under dispute

After leading Material Design at Google, Itai Vonshak argued that design systems had failed their broad promise to accelerate cohesive, high-quality, consistent product design at scale. In his account, the field became preoccupied with components, tokens, documentation, and adherence while accumulating maintenance cost, adoption friction, and visual sameness.1 This is a critique of the dominant institutional form of design systems, not comparative proof that every kind of shared system fails.

Brad Frost's broader definition makes the target clearer: a design system is the “official story” of how an organization designs and builds interfaces, carried through a design library, code library, and reference site.4 Vonshak's challenge is whether publishing and enforcing that story creates the outcomes it promises.

Adherence is not product quality

Vonshak's adherence fallacy is the strongest part of the critique: a product is not good because it religiously follows a system. Thoughtful problem framing, desirability, usability, content, and contextual fit cannot be delegated to component compliance.1 Much of the wider corpus agrees. Mall treats consistency as a by-product rather than a sufficient value proposition,2 while the quality literature separates the quality of system outputs from the end-user product experience the system can influence but not own.10

Wiki position: a system can raise a quality floor for repeated implementation concerns; it cannot establish the quality ceiling for the product. Compliance shows that selected constraints were satisfied, not that the right experience was designed. See Success Metrics and Measuring Design Systems.

Documentation is not dead; disconnected documentation is

Vonshak calls pattern documentation an “unread novel” when it must be found, read, remembered, and translated but is not encapsulated in code.1 Frost's reference site remains useful as an authoritative record,4 but Mall supplies the sharper response: documenting intent is insufficient when the implementation permits something else. APIs, type constraints, validated token pipelines, and layout components should encode settled boundaries while prose preserves rationale and uncertainty.3

Nathan Curtis adds a third representation: structured component specs can make props, anatomy, defaults, bindings, variants, and constraints inspectable by humans and machines without forcing either to infer intent from screenshots or raw tool data.9 The durable model is therefore three-part: prose for reasoning, executable contracts for rules, and structured data for precise exchange. See Components as Data.

Adoption is an internal product-market-fit test

Vonshak asks whether design systems have reached internal product-market fit at all: system teams spend heavily on persuasion, while full adoption imposes workflow, migration, training, and creative costs that code reuse alone may not justify.1 Mall's response concedes the friction. Product teams exist to ship products, not maintain central infrastructure; adoption and contribution must become the path of least resistance, and an API that is harder to integrate than rebuild will lose.23

Not all continued selling proves failed fit. Callahan argues that internal infrastructure needs ongoing translation because its revenue impact is indirect and different sponsors value efficiency, onboarding, accessibility, trust, or scale differently.7 The key distinction is between translation—explaining indirect value to funders—and compensation—repeatedly persuading users to tolerate an inferior workflow. The latter is evidence of poor fit. See Adoption, Contribution & Trust.

A bounded center instead of universal rules

Vonshak identifies two scale failures: predefined boxes can redirect attention from the user problem to the available pattern, and a centralized answer across many products can become a compromise that fits none of them well.1 Token architecture answers part—but not all—of the objection. Separating shared structure from aesthetic tokens can let brands and themes vary over a common component core; it does not prove that one interaction model or product flow fits every context.5

The organizational sources support a bounded center: centralize stable, repeated substrate; keep contextual decisions at the product edge; provide graduated escape paths; and treat repeated exceptions as evidence rather than automatic violations. Zhang's maturity model combines constrained configuration, slots, and headless layers with system-owned migrations and trust-building at scale,6 while Mall treats detached components and inventor–maintainer tension as feedback about gaps and unsettled needs.3

The economics are contingent

Small teams searching for product-market fit may not have enough stable repetition to justify formal infrastructure; large organizations can gain reach while paying substantial coordination and maintenance costs. Frost advises starting simply and adding complexity only as need appears,4 Mall argues for scope before scale and systems that emerge from product work,2 and Zhang shows the potential upside when a mature system can propagate a core change across many consumers.6

Wiki heuristic: shared-system leverage rises with the frequency and reach of a repeated decision and the cost of getting it wrong or changing it later. System burden rises with maintenance, coordination, adoption, migration, and lost local fit. A fuller system is justified only while expected leverage persistently exceeds that burden; “full adoption” is not a universal good.

AI can repair or amplify the model

Vonshak's proposed next chapter is dynamic, intelligent, adaptable design that remains consistent where needed.1 His article poses that as an agenda, not a demonstrated architecture. Frost's agentic response is more conservative: make the existing system machine-readable through examples, metadata, constraints, functional/accessibility/visual tests, and human sign-off.8

These approaches point toward different futures: compliance automation, where agents assemble interfaces correctly from existing decisions, and contextual adaptation, where agents vary experiences within bounded product and user contexts. Machine readability can strengthen either, but can also scale a bad constraint faster. A contingent architecture places stable tokens, behavior, accessibility constraints, and provenance below; bounded agent mediation above; and evaluation plus human judgment around both.

Perspectives: reduction, repair, or transformation?

  • Reduce the claim (Vonshak): design systems are one tool, not the solution to design at scale. Adherence and full adoption are not defensible universal goals, and static systems leave important adaptive work undone.1
  • Repair the operating model (Mall, Zhang, Callahan): many failures are failures of scope, fit, service, trust, maintenance, and value translation. Start from real product work, earn dependence through reliability, and use workarounds as feedback.2367
  • Transform the representation (Frost, Curtis): the system remains useful, but its assets must become flexible, structured, executable, and machine-readable so people and agents can act on intent rather than merely read it.589

Wiki position: the critique defeats design systems as universal rulebooks and guarantors of greatness. It does not defeat selectively shared infrastructure. The defensible system standardizes settled, high-leverage decisions while keeping product judgment, exceptions, and evolution alive.

The replacement promise

A design system can reasonably promise to reduce the cost of repeated decisions, raise the reliability floor for common concerns, make selected changes easier to propagate, preserve organizational reasoning, and expose explicit boundaries and escape paths. It cannot promise to identify the right user problem, guarantee usability or desirability, remove politics and maintenance, fit every product context, justify full adoption everywhere, or turn compliance into design excellence.

A design system should make thoughtful product work easier and safer; it cannot do the thinking on the product team's behalf.
